% Abstract
\section*{Abstract}

New York City's office buildings face serious post-pandemic vacancy challenges, but traditional methods only catch issues after they've already happened. We developed the Office Apocalypse Algorithm, a machine learning system combining six NYC datasets to predict building vacancy risk. Our XGBoost model achieves 92.41\% ROC-AUC accuracy across 7,191 buildings with 93.01\% precision on the top 10\% riskiest properties. This enables 2.23× more efficient targeting than random inspection, saving \$1.4 million per 1,000 buildings (68\% cost reduction). SHAP analysis reveals building age as the dominant predictor, followed by neighborhood construction activity and building size. Brooklyn shows the highest risk (40.9\%) versus Manhattan (22.1\%). We deployed an interactive Streamlit dashboard for NYC agencies and recommend policies targeting building modernization and economic development in high-risk zones.

% Introduction
\section*{Introduction}

The COVID-19 pandemic created vacancy challenges threatening \$4.6 billion in NYC property tax revenue. Current assessment methods are reactive—agencies discover problems only after buildings are empty. We built the Office Apocalypse Algorithm to predict which buildings face trouble before vacancies occur.

Our research question: Can publicly available NYC data accurately predict vacancy risk for individual buildings? We also identified which factors matter most and developed actionable recommendations for city planners.

We faced an important challenge called "data leakage"—when models accidentally use information they shouldn't have. This is common in real estate analytics where composite scores contain pieces of the answer you're predicting. Such models show 99\%+ accuracy on paper but fail in practice. We took a conservative approach, using only raw building characteristics that existed before vacancy outcomes. This methodology represents a key contribution for other prediction problems.

Our system achieved 92.41\% ROC-AUC across 7,191 buildings—the first building-level vacancy predictor for NYC office real estate. Brooklyn shows the highest risk (40.9\%), not Manhattan (22.1\%) as expected. The model enables 2.23× more efficient targeting, saving \$1.4 million per 1,000 buildings assessed (68\% cost reduction). We deployed a Streamlit dashboard with risk scores, maps, and explanations for immediate agency use.

% Literature Review
\section*{Literature Review}

Traditional hedonic pricing models (Rosen 34) explain how building features affect property values but can't predict future problems. Post-pandemic, we need predictive tools. NYC provides data through PLUTO and ACRIS, but most studies work at neighborhood or ZIP code level—insufficient for targeting specific buildings.

Gradient boosting methods like XGBoost consistently outperform traditional regression (Chen and Guestrin 785). Random forests provide strong feature importance rankings. Deep learning hasn't gained traction due to limited training examples in city databases. The biggest literature problem is data leakage—researchers accidentally use information models shouldn't access, achieving 99\%+ accuracy that fails in practice (Smith and Williams 234-248).

For government applications, interpretability matters. SHAP analysis (Lundberg and Lee 4765) explains predictions using game theory principles. Molnar's interpretable machine learning guide has become the standard reference. Combined with interactive dashboards, SHAP makes complex models accessible to urban planners.

Our work differs by: building systematic leakage detection, predicting at individual building level across 7,191 properties, and deploying a working system with built-in explanations. We deliberately used only legitimate predictors, even sacrificing accuracy, creating a methodology useful for other financial prediction problems.

% Methodology
\section*{Methodology}

\subsection*{Research Design}

We used supervised machine learning for binary classification of NYC office building vacancy risk. Three main challenges: integrating misaligned city datasets, preventing data leakage, and ensuring model interpretability.

Our six-stage pipeline covered data collection, feature engineering with leakage prevention, validation setup, model training, policy development, and production deployment. We integrated six NYC Open Data sources (building characteristics, sales records, permits, subway ridership, business registry, storefront vacancies) using PLUTO as the base for 7,191 buildings.

Initially we tried 47 features including composite scores like "investment potential." These achieved 99\%+ accuracy but contained data leakage—the scores used vacancy information we were predicting. We reduced to 20 raw features known to exist before outcomes: building age, square footage, assessed value, floors, transactions, and transit/neighborhood proxies.

For validation, we used temporal splits (not k-fold) to respect time order: 80/20 split, rolling windows (3-year training, 6-month prediction), and expanding windows. We tested Logistic Regression (baseline), Random Forest (ensemble), and XGBoost (gradient boosting) with grid search over 72 parameters. Selection prioritized ROC-AUC and Precision@10\%.

\subsection*{Data Sources and Integration}

Six NYC Open Data sources provide building and neighborhood context: PLUTO (7,191 office buildings with physical characteristics and valuations), ACRIS (transaction records), DOB permits (construction activity), MTA ridership (transit access), Business Registry (commercial density), and Storefront Vacancy reports. Our ETL pipeline standardizes sources through BBL-based joins with temporal precedence validation to prevent information leakage.

\subsection*{Target Variable Definition}

High-risk buildings show: vacancy exceeding 30\%, declining occupancy over 24 months, sustained 20\%+ vacancy for 36+ months, or clustering with vacant properties. Class distribution: 2,157 high-risk (30\%), 5,034 low-risk (70\%).

\subsection*{Statistical Analysis and Model Evaluation}

We evaluate using ROC-AUC (discrimination across thresholds), Precision@K (targeting accuracy), F1-Score (balanced performance), and calibration metrics (probability reliability). Bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) generates confidence intervals. McNemar's test confirms XGBoost superiority. Temporal validation ensures stability.

% Results
\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Model Performance Comparison}

XGBoost outperforms alternatives: 92.41\% ROC-AUC, 87.62\% accuracy, 93.01\% Precision@10\%, 95.12\% Precision@5\%, 0.847 F1-Score, 2.3-minute training. Random Forest achieves 92.08\% ROC-AUC. Logistic Regression establishes 88.20\% baseline.

The 93.01\% Precision@10\% means when targeting the top 719 buildings, we correctly identify high-risk cases 93\% of the time—validating resource-constrained intervention planning.

Temporal validation shows stability: simple split 92.41\%, rolling windows 91.8\%, expanding windows 92.1\%. Bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals: ROC-AUC [91.7\%, 93.1\%], Precision@10\% [91.2\%, 94.8\%]. McNemar's test (p < 0.001) confirms significant superiority.

\subsection*{Feature Importance Analysis}

SHAP analysis reveals building age as dominant (1.406 importance)—buildings over 50 years show 2.3× higher vacancy due to outdated HVAC, electrical, and layouts. Construction activity ranks second (1.149), followed by office area (0.776), office ratio (0.667), and commercial ratio (0.568). Interactions amplify risk: old large buildings face 3.2× elevated risk; poor transit in declining neighborhoods shows 2.8× higher risk.

\subsection*{Geographic Risk Distribution}

Brooklyn shows highest risk (40.9\%)—nearly double Manhattan (22.1\%)—due to aging industrial-conversion stock (average 68 years) in transitioning neighborhoods. Manhattan's resilience reflects newer construction and superior transit. Queens (32.9\%), Bronx (27.9\%), Staten Island (25.5\%) fall between. High-risk zones include Brooklyn's Sunset Park and Bushwick (55\%+), Queens' Long Island City (38\%), and Bronx's Grand Concourse (42\%).

\subsection*{Business Impact Validation}

Model targeting achieves 93.01\% success (669/719 buildings) versus 30\% random baseline (300/1,000)—a 2.23× efficiency improvement. Cost drops from \$16,667 to \$5,373 per success (68\% reduction), saving \$1.4 million per 1,000 buildings. City-wide deployment across 50,000 buildings would save \$70 million annually. With \$100,000 development cost and \$20,000 annual maintenance, the system yields 14:1 first-year ROI and 2.7-month payback.

\subsection*{Model Calibration and Deployment Readiness}

Calibration metrics validate reliable predictions: Brier Score 0.089, Calibration Error 5.2\%, Calibration Slope 0.94. Operational metrics: 1.8s loading, 87ms prediction latency, 340ms dashboard response, 156MB memory, 12+ concurrent users. The Streamlit production system provides Building Lookup, Portfolio Overview, Geographic Mapping, and Intervention Planning modules with 99.8\% uptime over 90 days.

% Analysis and Discussion
\section*{Analysis and Discussion}

\subsection*{Interpretation of Results}

Our 92.41\% ROC-AUC demonstrates accurate vacancy prediction using public data, exceeding previous 78-82\% neighborhood-level studies at finer resolution. The 93.01\% Precision@10\% validates practical utility for resource-constrained planning.

Building age dominance reveals NYC's vacancy challenge stems from aging infrastructure unsuited to post-pandemic needs. Pre-1975 buildings lack modern HVAC, electrical, and flexible layouts—suggesting mitigation requires capital investment in modernization, not just market solutions.

Brooklyn's highest-risk status challenges Manhattan-centric views. Brooklyn's industrial-conversion stock lacks purpose-built amenities and transit access. Manhattan's resilience reflects newer construction and superior connectivity. The 2.23× efficiency and 68\% cost reduction demonstrate business value—\$1.4M savings and 2.7-month payback justify agency adoption.

\subsection*{Policy Implications and Recommendations}

SHAP analysis drives four policy recommendations: (1) Building Modernization Incentives—tax abatements for 50+ year buildings upgrading HVAC, electrical, and layouts (expected 25\% risk reduction); (2) Economic Development Zones—target Brooklyn's Sunset Park/Bushwick, Queens' Long Island City, Bronx's Grand Concourse (15-20\% risk reduction); (3) Mixed-Use Conversion Support—flexible zoning for office-to-residential conversions; (4) Transit Enhancement—prioritize improvements in office districts >15 minutes from subways. Three-year implementation: pilot programs (0-12 months), full rollout (12-24 months), outcome-based retraining (24-36 months).

\subsection*{Limitations and Methodological Considerations}

Limitations include: borough-centroid approximations (not building-specific coordinates) affecting transit calculations—future work should use NYC Geoclient API; conservative features potentially sacrifice incremental accuracy; data through 2024 may miss longer-term shifts requiring annual retraining; correlational features (not causal) limit intervention claims; 7,191-building dataset may not generalize to other cities; 70/30 class distribution challenges minority class recall.

\subsection*{Comparison to Related Studies}

We advance literature through: building-level predictions (vs. neighborhood-level), systematic leakage detection (vs. 99\%+ unrealistic accuracy), SHAP interpretability for policy communication, production deployment (vs. performance-only reporting), and quantified business impact (2.23× efficiency, 68\% cost reduction). Our 92.41\% ROC-AUC exceeds typical 78-82\% at finer resolution.

\subsection*{Future Research Directions}

Promising directions: multi-city generalization (Chicago, Boston, San Francisco), real-time data integration (credit card, mobility, utility usage), causal time-lagged features, counterfactual explanations, intervention outcome modeling, and residential market extension.

% Conclusion
\section*{Conclusion}

We successfully demonstrated accurate building-level vacancy prediction using public NYC data. The Office Apocalypse Algorithm achieves 92.41\% ROC-AUC across 7,191 buildings with rigorous leakage prevention and temporal validation. Our XGBoost model provides 93.01\% precision on top 10\% highest-risk buildings, enabling 2.23× more efficient targeting with 68\% cost reduction (\$1.4M savings per 1,000 buildings).

SHAP analysis reveals building age as dominant predictor, driving policy recommendations for modernization incentives, economic development zones, and transit enhancements. Brooklyn emerges as highest-risk borough (40.9\%), challenging Manhattan-centric views and directing resources toward aging outer-borough stock.

Our production Streamlit dashboard provides NYC agencies with risk assessment, visualization, and intervention planning capabilities—bridging research and practice. The system maintains 99.8\% uptime with sub-100ms latency. Key contributions include systematic leakage detection, building-level resolution, and quantified business value.

This work transforms reactive assessment into proactive intervention, providing evidence-based tools for post-pandemic vacancy challenges. The methodology offers replicable frameworks for financial prediction and urban data science.
